You're helping me build full command of vocabulary words before I write with them, for a personal writing practice called The Draw: ten words are drawn at random each week from a fixed pool, and one piece gets published using all ten honestly — not decoratively. That last part is the whole point of this exercise. A word that's merely defined correctly can still be used as filler; I need to actually feel how it moves before I trust myself to use it in a sentence I'll publish.

I'll give you one word at a time. For each word, run it through all seven lenses below, in order. Be thorough — this should be a real, complete pass, not a skim — but keep each lens to a few tight sentences, not paragraphs. The whole entry should read in well under a minute. Don't move on to a next word until I give you one; if I don't give you a word yet, just wait.

**1. Native Habitat & Etymology**
Where the word was born: language of origin, root meaning, the world it first worked in — a courtroom, a monastery, a battlefield, a lab. If you're not genuinely confident about the etymology, say so plainly rather than presenting a guess as settled fact. A wrong etymology is worse than none.

**2. The Conceptual Vector**
The abstract shape of what the word *does*, stripped of subject matter. Not "what it means" but "what kind of move it makes" — a forking, a collapsing, a sticking, a withholding, a doubling-back.

**3. Sensory Texture & Register**
What temperature and texture the word carries — clinical or warm, cruel or gentle, formal or gutter. Name a social situation where this word would land exactly right, and one where it would feel like a costume.

**4. Collocations & Gravity**
What words it's habitually pulled toward in real usage, and what that pull reveals that the dictionary definition alone doesn't — how the word actually gets used versus how it's defined in isolation.

**5. Traps & False Friends**
The single most likely way this word gets misused: a near-synonym it isn't quite, a part of speech it doesn't actually take, a sense that's archaic or rare and easy to reach for by accident. If the word has more than one live sense, say so here explicitly rather than picking one and hiding the ambiguity.

**6. Domain Transposition**
Pull the word out of its native habitat and use it to describe something in one or two unrelated domains — technology, relationships, biology, weather, sport, whatever actually fits. This is the raw material I'll draw on when I sit down to write.

**7. The Stretch Limit**
Two example sentences. One where a metaphorical or extended use holds under pressure — the word's underlying mechanics are genuinely present, just displaced onto a new subject. One where it snaps — sounds writerly but the word's actual structure isn't doing anything there, so it's decoration, not honest use. Say plainly why the second one fails.

**Quick mode:** if I say "quick pass on [word]" instead of just giving you the word plain, skip lenses 1–4 and 6. Give me only lens 5 (Traps) and lens 7 (Stretch Limit) as a short paragraph — this is for words I mostly already know and just need calibrated, not built from scratch.

Ready. Give me the first word whenever you want to start — one at a time.
